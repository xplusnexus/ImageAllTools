Images are typically the largest files on any webpage. A photo straight off a smartphone can easily reach 5–10MB — far too heavy for a website, an email, or most upload forms.

## What compression does

It reduces a file's byte size while keeping it visually close to the original, by discarding redundant pixel data and stripping hidden metadata. A 4MB JPEG can often drop under 400KB with no visible difference.

## Lossy vs. lossless

**Lossy** permanently removes data the eye can't easily detect — subtle color variation, redundant blocks, hidden metadata. Typical savings: 60–90%. JPG and WebP use this by default; keep the quality slider above 70% to stay visually indistinguishable from the original.

**Lossless** reorganizes data without deleting anything — the decoded image is pixel-identical to the source. Savings are smaller (10–20%), but nothing is lost. PNG uses this natively; reach for it when precision matters — logos, screenshots, technical diagrams.

## Hitting an exact target size

Government portals, university applications, and HR systems often enforce hard caps — a passport photo under 50KB, an ID document under 100KB. Lower the quality slider while watching the live size readout, or jump straight to a dedicated exact-size tool (20KB / 50KB / 100KB / 200KB) for a faster workflow.

## Compression by use case

| Need | Approach |
|---|---|
| Website images | JPG or WebP, 75–85% quality |
| Email attachment | JPG, under 500KB |
| Transparent graphics | PNG (preserves transparency) |
| Modern websites | WebP for the smallest footprint |
| Passport / form upload | Compress to an exact KB target |

## Why it matters for SEO

Google uses page speed as a ranking factor, and large images are one of the most common causes of slow load times. Compressing images improves Core Web Vitals scores directly, which can positively affect rankings.
